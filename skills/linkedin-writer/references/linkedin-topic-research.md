# LinkedIn Topic Research and Conversation Map

Use this reference before outlining a LinkedIn Article, newsletter edition, or thought-leadership series. Its purpose is to find a professionally relevant conversation with enough evidence and specificity to support a useful article.

## Research Principle

LinkedIn topic research is not a hunt for the largest hashtag. The useful signal is the intersection of:

1. a recurring professional problem or decision;
2. a current change, tension, or misconception;
3. an audience the author can credibly help;
4. evidence or experience strong enough to support a point of view;
5. a question that qualified readers may want to discuss.

Search frequency, feed reach, and business relevance are different things. Do not treat one as proof of another.

## Source Hierarchy

Use the strongest available sources in this order:

1. User-provided customer questions, approved professional experience, support themes, sales objections, or internal research.
2. Primary platform, product, policy, standards, research, regulatory, or company sources.
3. LinkedIn's own search suggestions and filtered Posts results.
4. Original reports and credible industry datasets.
5. Attributable practitioner posts that add experience or disagreement.
6. Google results restricted to public LinkedIn Posts, Articles/Pulse pages, newsletters, and Company pages as conversation-discovery aids.
7. General web search, community summaries, and third-party trend lists as discovery aids only.

Do not use a search snippet as evidence when the original page can be opened. Do not copy the structure or conclusion of a high-performing post.

## LinkedIn Search Procedure

When LinkedIn search is available:

1. Enter the core phrase in the LinkedIn search bar.
2. Record type-ahead suggestions as discovery signals, not search-volume data.
3. Open the **Posts** category.
4. Compare recent and top results when the interface exposes sorting.
5. Apply useful filters such as date posted, content type, author industry, author company, mentioning member, or mentioning company.
6. Repeat with a natural-language problem or question, not only a keyword.
7. Inspect a small, relevant sample deeply enough to identify repeated questions, disagreements, examples, and vocabulary.
8. Search the same topic under **People**, **Companies**, **Groups**, **Events**, **Courses**, or **Products** only when that surface matters to the article.

LinkedIn search supports keywords and natural-language queries. Hashtags can be searched, but suggested searches do not necessarily surface hashtags. Treat hashtags as optional topic labels, not the main research method.

## Google Search for LinkedIn Content

Use Google when LinkedIn search is inaccessible, personalized, too narrow, or when the article needs a broader sample of publicly indexed LinkedIn conversations.

Start with:

```text
site:linkedin.com/posts "<topic>" "<professional role>"
site:linkedin.com/posts "<topic>" "<business outcome or objection>" after:YYYY-MM-DD
site:linkedin.com/pulse "<topic>" "<implementation, ROI, risk, or governance>"
site:linkedin.com/company "<topic>" "<official case, launch, or report>"
site:linkedin.com "<exact phrase>" -jobs -learning
```

Google officially supports quoted exact phrases, `site:`, exclusions with `-`, and `after:`/`before:` date operators. Do not put a space after `site:`.

For each useful result, record the query, date, URL, result type, author role, visible date, directly observed professional question or tension, and whether the original page was opened. Treat snippets as discovery text only. A LinkedIn post or Article may supply practitioner experience or a competing interpretation, but material factual claims still require primary or authoritative verification.

## Topic Expansion Lenses

Use these lenses to enrich one article without losing focus:

### Professional outcome

What changes in cost, speed, quality, growth, hiring, learning, risk, customer value, or decision quality?

### Role impact

What does the topic mean for an individual contributor, manager, executive, buyer, candidate, operator, or specialist?

### Workflow

What happens before, during, and after adoption? What prerequisites, handoffs, review points, or metrics matter?

### Trade-off

Which desirable outcomes conflict? What does the popular advice ignore?

### Evidence

Which official data, original report, documented product behavior, or attributable example can support the article?

### Failure mode

Where does the method break? What is expensive, slow, risky, hard to measure, or inappropriate?

### Decision

What should the reader choose, stop, start, measure, or discuss with their team?

### Career and skill signal

Which capabilities, portfolio evidence, communication patterns, or leadership behaviors become more valuable?

## Topic Candidate Scorecard

Score each candidate from 1 to 5:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Audience relevance | Does a defined professional reader face this problem now? |
| Timeliness | Is there a verified change, active tension, or current trigger? |
| Authority fit | Can this author credibly contribute evidence, analysis, or approved experience? |
| Evidence strength | Are reliable sources or attributable examples available? |
| Practical value | Can the reader make a decision or improve a workflow? |
| Distinctiveness | Is there a non-obvious thesis, trade-off, or synthesis? |
| Conversation potential | Can qualified readers answer a specific question? |
| Series value | Does this open a coherent follow-up topic without padding? |

Reject a topic if authority fit or evidence strength is below 3, even when it appears timely.

## Research Stop Rule

Stop topic research when all of the following are true:

- the professional audience and reader decision are specific;
- one thesis is stronger than the alternatives;
- 3-6 material claims have viable evidence paths;
- related topics add depth without changing the subject;
- one real counterargument or limitation is visible;
- the article can end with a concrete professional question.

More browsing after this point often adds noise rather than value.
